The problem: A bill that keeps rising, and nobody can say where it goes.

Electricity is the largest operating cost most commercial and industrial building owners don't actively manage. It arrives as one number at the end of the month. Nobody can tell you which plant, which board or which hour of the night drove it.

Meanwhile the price keeps climbing. NERSA approved a further 9.01% increase in what Eskom charges municipalities from July 2026, and it flows straight through to municipal tariffs. The US EPA estimates that around 30% of the energy used in commercial buildings is wasted. On a R200,000 monthly bill, that could be R60,000 a month paying for nothing.

Roughly 0.94 kg of CO₂ for every kWh drawn from South Africa's grid (national grid emission factor, 2023). For most commercial buildings, wasted electricity is the largest single part of the carbon footprint.

Where the money leaks

  • After-hours base load. Air conditioning, lighting, compressors, extraction and process equipment running at night and over weekends when nobody is there.
  • Maximum demand peaks. On a kVA demand tariff, one badly timed start-up or a few units switching on together can set the demand charge for the whole month.
  • Poor power factor. Reactive energy charges and inflated kVA demand for current that does no useful work. We have taken a site from 0.91 to 0.99 and removed the penalties.
  • HVAC and refrigeration. Wrong setpoints, simultaneous heating and cooling, poor chiller sequencing, and refrigeration controlled on air temperature instead of product temperature.
  • Compressed air. Leaks, over-pressure and compressors loading and unloading all day. Often the most expensive utility on an industrial site.
  • Motors, pumps and fans. Oversized for the duty, throttled instead of speed-controlled, running whether the process needs them or not.
  • The electrical network itself. Overloaded cables, lightly loaded or ageing transformers, harmonics from drives and electronics, and phase imbalance, all turning paid-for energy into heat.
  • Lighting and controls. Old fittings, no occupancy or daylight control, and timers nobody has checked since commissioning.

How an Energy Loss Audit works

  1. Bills and tariff review. Twelve to twenty-four months of electricity accounts. We check your tariff structure, time-of-use periods, notified maximum demand, reactive energy charges and whether you are on the right tariff at all.
  2. Site walk-down. We trace the main loads with the people who run the building: Plant rooms, kitchens, compressors, cold rooms, production lines and the switchroom.
  3. Measure. Power quality analysers on the main incomer and key distribution boards, usually for one to two weeks, so the load profile reflects real weekdays, nights and weekends.
  4. Load flow and loss analysis. Where the energy goes, board by board and hour by hour, and where the electrical network itself loses it through cables, transformers, harmonics and imbalance.
  5. Feasibility study. Each saving measure costed and ranked: Expected kWh and kVA savings, capital cost, payback period, carbon reduction and the effect on any planned solar or battery system.
  6. Proposal and verification plan. A plan you can take to the board, with a measurement and verification method agreed up front so savings are proven against the baseline, not promised.

Energy audit questions

What is an energy audit?

An energy audit measures how a building uses electricity, finds where it is wasted and sets out what to do about it. Our Energy Loss Audit combines a bills and tariff review, on-site power quality logging, a load flow and loss analysis and a feasibility study, ending in a costed, ranked plan.

How much can an energy audit save?

It depends on the site, which is why we measure before we promise. The US EPA estimates that around 30% of the energy used in commercial buildings is wasted, and tariff and power factor fixes alone often pay for the audit. The report gives you a savings figure for your site with the evidence behind it.

How long does an energy audit take?

Typically three to five weeks from the site visit to the report, depending on the size of the site. Most of that is one to two weeks of logging so the load profile covers real weekdays, nights and weekends.

What do you need from us?

Twelve to twenty-four months of electricity accounts, single-line diagrams if you have them, safe access to the main switchroom and distribution boards, and time with someone who knows how the site runs.

Should we do an energy audit before installing solar?

Yes. A PV system sized on a wasteful load is bigger and slower to pay back. Removing the waste first means fewer panels and a smaller battery for the same result.

Does an energy audit help with carbon reporting?

Yes. The audit gives you a measured baseline in kWh and tonnes of CO2, using South Africa's national grid emission factor, and a way to verify reductions over time for ESG reporting, tenants, lenders and export customers.
