Maximum demand charges in South Africa: How to cut your kVA peak in 2026/27.

By Bramwill Bruinders, Pr. Tech. Eng. · 8 November 2026 · 9 minute read

In short

  • A demand charge is set by the single highest 30-minute period of the month, not by how much energy you use.
  • On the City of Cape Town's 2026/27 low-voltage large-user tariff, every kVA of that peak costs R254.76 a month, excluding VAT.
  • Your notified maximum demand (NMD) is a second, separate cost: Set it too high and you pay for capacity you never use; exceed it and penalties follow.
  • Most peaks are made by things starting at the same time. Staggering start-ups is often free and is usually the first fix.
  • Measure before you buy anything: Demand limiting, power factor correction and batteries only pay back when they are sized on your real load profile.

Many commercial and industrial electricity accounts carry a charge that has little to do with how much electricity the site uses. It is set by one half-hour in the month, often a Monday morning when the air conditioning, compressors, kitchen and lights all come on together. For the rest of the month the site never comes close to that level again, but the account charges for it anyway.

That is the maximum demand charge. It is one of the quickest wins in an energy audit, because the peak is usually created by timing rather than by need, and timing can often be changed for free. This guide explains how demand is measured and billed under 2026/27 tariffs, what your notified maximum demand costs you, and how to bring the peak down without disrupting operations.

What a maximum demand charge is

Your site's demand is the rate at which it draws power at any moment, measured in kVA (or kW on some tariffs). Utilities bill it because they have to build cables, transformers and substations big enough for your highest demand, however briefly you reach it.

Eskom defines maximum demand as the highest average demand during any 30-minute integrating period in a billing month. A motor starting for a few seconds won't set it, but a half-hour in which everything runs together will. Municipal large-user tariffs bill demand in the same spirit: One high period in the month sets the charge for the whole month.

What it costs in 2026/27

City of Cape Town large users

The City's 2026/27 tariffs, effective from 1 July 2026, bill large users two capacity charges per kVA, excluding VAT:

City of Cape Town large-user capacity charges, 2026/27, excluding VAT
Demand charge, low voltageR254.76 per kVA per month (up 8.55% from R234.69)
Demand charge, medium voltageR82.38 per kVA per month
Network capacity charge, low voltageR39.00 per kVA per month
Network capacity charge, medium voltageR19.15 per kVA per month

The demand charge follows your actual peak each month. The network capacity charge relates to the capacity reserved for your supply, your notified maximum demand, so you pay it whether or not you use that capacity.

Eskom Megaflex

Eskom splits capacity charges more finely. On Megaflex in 2026/27, for a supply between 500 V and 66 kV within 300 km of Johannesburg, the charges excluding VAT include:

  • Network demand charge, R26.29 per kVA per month, on the chargeable demand: The highest 30-minute demand in peak and standard periods only. Demand you create in off-peak hours doesn't count towards it.
  • Distribution network capacity charge, R39.13 per kVA per month, plus a Transmission network charge of R11.15, on your annual utilised capacity: The higher of your NMD and your maximum demand.

Below 500 V the Megaflex network demand charge is R52.65 per kVA, so small Eskom-supplied sites feel their peaks even more.

Your notified maximum demand: Two ways to overpay

Your notified maximum demand (NMD) is the capacity you have contracted with the utility for your supply. It is a separate decision from your actual peak, and it can cost you in two opposite ways.

Set too high: Paying for capacity you never use

Many sites still carry an NMD sized for a production line, chiller or tenant that is long gone. If your NMD is 800 kVA and your real peak is 520 kVA, the City's network capacity charge on the unused 280 kVA is about R10,920 a month at R39.00 per kVA, or roughly R131,000 a year, before VAT. Ask your utility how and when an NMD can be reduced. Changes are governed by the NMD rules, and some utilities limit how often you can change it.

Set too low: Excess charges every time you go over

On Eskom tariffs, going above your NMD triggers an excess network capacity charge: The kVA above the NMD, multiplied by the event number, multiplied by the excess rate. On Megaflex between 500 V and 66 kV within 300 km, the excess rate is R50.28 per kVA in 2026/27. A site with a 500 kVA NMD that reaches 560 kVA pays 60 × R50.28 ≈ R3,017 for that first exceedance. Because the event number rises each time the NMD is exceeded, a repeat costs double, then triple. An NMD that is regularly exceeded is a sign the supply needs reviewing, not a cost to live with.

What creates the peak

  • Everything starting at once: Monday mornings, after a public holiday, and especially when power returns after an outage and every load restarts together.
  • HVAC pulling down a hot building: Chillers and packaged units all starting at full load in summer, or electric heating doing the same on a winter morning.
  • Compressed air and pumping: Several compressors loading together, or pumps all called at once to fill tanks.
  • Kitchens and process heat: Ovens, fryers, kettles, furnaces and heating elements switched on at the start of a shift.
  • New loads nobody planned for: Electric vehicle chargers, battery chargers and new equipment added without checking the supply.
  • Poor power factor: On a kVA tariff, every reactive kVA adds to the billed peak. Our power factor guide explains how to fix it.

How to cut the peak, cheapest fixes first

  1. Get the 30-minute data. Ask your utility for your half-hourly meter data, or log your main incomer for at least two weeks, including a Monday start-up. You can't manage a peak you haven't seen.
  2. Stagger start-ups. Delay HVAC units, compressors, pumps and heating by a few minutes each using the timers and controls you already have, so they don't all reach full load in the same half-hour. Program a staged restart for when power returns after an outage.
  3. Shift flexible loads out of the peak. Water heating, pumping into storage, defrost cycles, charging and batch processes can often run before or after the busiest half-hour. On Eskom Megaflex, demand in off-peak periods doesn't count towards the network demand charge at all.
  4. Correct the power factor. On a kVA tariff, raising the power factor from 0.85 to 0.98 can cut the billed peak by about 13% with no change to how the site runs.
  5. Add demand limiting. A controller that watches the incoming demand and briefly sheds non-critical loads, such as water heaters or a second compressor, when the half-hour average approaches a set target.
  6. Consider a battery for peak shaving, last. A battery can cover the peak, but only if it is sized on your measured profile and its controller discharges against the demand target every time the peak forms. Price it against what the cheaper steps above already save.
  7. Review your NMD once a year. When the peak comes down, reduce the NMD to match, leaving sensible headroom for growth.

Why solar alone rarely cuts the demand charge

A rooftop PV system cuts the energy you buy, but a demand charge is set by a single half-hour. A passing cloud, a start-up before the sun is high, or a winter evening peak can set the month's demand even on a site with a large array. Solar can still help when the peak sits in the middle of a sunny day, but don't count on it in a demand calculation unless a battery and controller are there to guarantee the reduction. This is one more reason to cut the waste and the peak first, then size the solar on what is left. We explain that order in our guide to energy audits before solar.

Questions to ask a peak-shaving or battery supplier

  • Was the system sized on our half-hourly meter data, or on our monthly bills?
  • What demand target will it hold, and what happens if the battery is empty when the peak forms?
  • Which loads will the controller shed, for how long, and who decides?
  • How much of the saving could we get from staggering start-ups and demand limiting alone?
  • What will it save per month on our tariff, and how will we verify it on the bill?
  • Should our NMD change afterwards, and who will apply for that?

Frequently asked questions

What is a maximum demand charge?

A monthly charge per kVA based on the highest demand your site recorded during the billing month, rather than on how much energy you used. On the City of Cape Town's 2026/27 low-voltage large-user tariff it is R254.76 per kVA per month, excluding VAT.

How is maximum demand measured?

Eskom defines it as the highest average demand in kVA or kW during any 30-minute integrating period in the billing month. On Megaflex, the demand that attracts the network demand charge is measured only in peak and standard periods. Check your own tariff's rules, because municipal tariffs differ.

What happens if my demand goes above my notified maximum demand (NMD)?

On Eskom tariffs you pay an excess network capacity charge: The kVA above your NMD, multiplied by the event number, multiplied by the excess charge rate. The event number rises each time the NMD is exceeded, so repeated exceedances cost progressively more.

Will solar panels reduce my maximum demand charge?

Not reliably. A demand charge is set by a single 30-minute period, and a passing cloud, an early-morning start-up or a winter evening peak can still set it. Solar mainly cuts kWh. To cut demand, control the loads or add a battery that is sized and programmed for the peak.

Can a battery cut my maximum demand?

Yes, if it is sized on a measured load profile and its controller discharges against the demand target every time the peak forms. Compare its cost with what cheaper fixes, such as staggered start-ups and demand limiting, already save.
