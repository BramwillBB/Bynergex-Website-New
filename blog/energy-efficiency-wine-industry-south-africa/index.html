Energy efficiency in South African wineries: Seven challenges, and the savings cellars already make.

By Bramwill Bruinders, Pr. Tech. Eng. · 12 November 2026 · 13 minute read

In short

  • The cost and structure of electricity tariffs, not load-shedding, is now the main energy risk for wineries. Many agricultural users saw effective increases of 25% to 30% in 2025, against a headline increase of 12.74%.
  • Cooling is the largest single electricity load in a cellar, and it peaks during harvest, in the hottest weeks of the year.
  • Pumping water to vineyards is the largest source of farm-level carbon emissions, so export buyers' carbon questions are really electricity questions.
  • South African cellars have already cut costs with low-capital measures: Warmer chilled water set points, smaller tanks out of season, fixing compressed air leaks and fitting variable speed drives.
  • Start with a tariff review and a harvest-season load profile. They show the cheap fixes first, and give you the evidence before any capital decision.

For most of the last decade, the energy question on a wine farm was how to keep the cellar running through load-shedding. That question has changed. In July 2026, Farmer's Weekly reported that electricity prices have replaced load-shedding as farming's biggest threat. For a winery, the question is now what each kilowatt-hour and each kVA costs, and when in the season the cellar uses them.

This guide is written for wine estates, co-operative cellars and producer cellars in the Western Cape. It covers where a winery uses its electricity, the seven energy challenges that matter most to profitability today, and the savings South African cellars have already achieved. Every figure links to its source, so you can check it and share it with your board, your cellar master or your bank.

Why the energy problem has changed

The National Energy Regulator of South Africa (NERSA) approved an average Eskom increase of 12.74% from 1 April 2025. The tariff restructuring that came with it hit rural users harder than the headline figure suggests. Energy Partners' analysis shows the restructuring added a further 8.4% to Ruraflex, Eskom's main rural time-of-use tariff, for an effective increase of about 21%. It also notes that the new fixed Generation Capacity Charge weighs more heavily on Ruraflex than on other tariffs.

On the farm, the effect was larger still. Farmer's Weekly reports effective increases of 25% to 30% for many agricultural users. In one AgriSA example, the annual cost of a 250 kVA irrigation supply rose from about R408,000 to R539,000 in a single season, an increase of 32%. The same article explains that a High Court judgment required NERSA to redetermine Eskom's allowable revenue, bringing the increases to 8.76% for 2026/27 and 8.83% for 2027/28.

Vinpro's production cost figures show the same pressure inside the wine industry. In its 2025 cost analysis, reported by Farmer's Weekly in August 2026, wine grape electricity costs rose by 14.6%, and water costs rose by 7.3%, driven partly by higher pumping costs.

A bigger share of the bill is now fixed. When fixed and capacity charges grow faster than energy charges, cutting kilowatt-hours alone saves less than many producers expect. That is why the first step in any winery energy project is to understand the tariff, not to buy equipment.

Where a winery uses its electricity

The wine industry's Confronting Climate Change (CCC) initiative publishes the best available energy benchmarks for South African wine. The CCC Benchmark Report 2025, published by SA Wine and in Wineland's October 2025 edition, uses combined data from 2011 to 2024 and gives these averages:

Average electricity use, CCC Benchmark Report 2025 (2011 to 2024 data)
Electricity useWhite wineRed wine
Cellar cooling (kWh per ton of grapes)232201
Cellar processing (kWh per ton of grapes)180174
Bottling (kWh per kilolitre of wine)229222
Vineyard (kWh per bearing hectare)1,7881,546

Cooling is the largest single load in the cellar, and it is higher for white wine, which is fermented colder. Earlier research by Stellenbosch University, published as a guideline for energy management in the South African wine industry (Brent, Silinga and Sanetra, 2016), estimated that refrigeration accounts for about half of a winery's energy demand, with the peak during harvest.

The same pattern shows up in carbon accounting. Because South Africa's grid electricity is mostly coal-based, the CCC initiative finds that electricity used to pump water to vineyards is the largest source of farm-level carbon emissions, while glass makes up most of the packaging emissions. In the cellar, electricity is the largest contributor.

Use these figures as a yardstick, not a target. If your cooling electricity per ton is well above the benchmark, the chilled water system is the first place to look. If it is close to the benchmark, the savings are more likely to come from the tariff and from when you run the load.

The seven most pressing challenges

The challenges below are ranked by their effect on cellar and farm profitability today. Most of them interact: A hotter harvest raises the cooling load, which raises the peak demand, which costs more under the new tariff structure.

1. Electricity cost and tariff structure

The figures above set the scene: An effective increase of 21% to 30% in 2025, a further 8.76% in 2026/27 and 8.83% in 2027/28, and a growing share of fixed and capacity charges. Ruraflex and municipal agricultural tariffs are complex, and a supply that was on the right tariff five years ago may not be on the right one now. On tariffs with capacity charges, the notified maximum demand (NMD) of each supply point is also worth checking. Our guide to maximum demand charges explains how capacity is billed and how to bring the peak down.

2. Refrigeration loads in a warming climate

Cooling demand peaks during harvest, in the hottest weeks of the year, and that peak drives both demand charges and the size of the refrigeration plant. The climate is moving against the cellar. Stellenbosch University's Prof. Nick Vink told Wineland that temperatures have risen by almost 2 °C and the harvest is about three weeks earlier than a few decades ago. In May 2026, Stellenbosch University viticulturist Dr Erna Blancquaert described the latest Western Cape harvest as the earliest ever. Hotter grapes arriving in a hotter cellar raise the cooling load per ton.

The refrigerant transition adds a deadline. From 2026, South Africa's R22 import quota falls to 2.5% of previous consumption, and CBN reports that operators still relying on R22 may run out of refrigerant within two years. Many cellars still run ageing R22 chillers. A like-for-like swap to a high-GWP HFC refrigerant such as R404A or R507 only defers the problem: Under the Kigali Amendment, South Africa has committed to cut HFC consumption by 80% over 30 years, with the first mandatory reduction due in 2029.

A chiller replacement forced by R22 is also the best chance in a generation to resize the plant, raise its efficiency and choose a refrigerant that will last. It deserves an independent specification, based on the cellar's measured cooling load rather than on the old plant's nameplate.

3. Irrigation pumping

About 80% of South African vineyards need irrigation, and Farmer's Weekly has noted that most of these pumping systems are outdated and not as energy-efficient as they should be, with variable speed drive pumps identified as one of the most valuable investments an irrigation farmer can make. Vinpro told Netafim that rising electricity costs have pushed up water management costs and changed irrigation investment decisions.

The coming season may make this worse. TimesLIVE reported in October 2026 that the South African Weather Service expects El Niño conditions to persist into 2027, with concerns about irrigation demand and heat stress, although the forecast does not apply evenly to the winter-rainfall Cape. Pumps that run longer on a tariff that costs more make pump efficiency, and the timing of pumping across time-of-use periods, a direct profit lever.

4. Carbon intensity and export market access

Because most of a cellar's carbon footprint comes from bought electricity, a buyer's carbon question is an energy question. Export buyers increasingly ask for carbon footprints, energy and water data, and lighter glass. A 2023 study by Das Nair, Chisoro and Ponte, Sustainability in the South African Wine Industry, notes that Sweden's state retailer Systembolaget is developing a tender for carbon-neutral wine. It also found that, as of 2022, only 39 of around 536 cellars and 17 of around 2,613 producers had completed a carbon footprint, and that retailers are generally not paying a premium to cover the cost of meeting their requirements.

Wine farms are not liable for the carbon tax itself. Cliffe Dekker Hofmeyr explains that the agriculture, forestry and other land use sector stays excluded under Phase 2, which started on 1 January 2026. The tax rate still rises each year to R462 per tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent by 2030, and the firm warns of unavoidable knock-on increases in fuel and energy costs.

5. Limited capital in a strained industry

An FTI Consulting report, covered by The Drinks Business in December 2025, found that only about 8% of producers were profitable in 2023, while 43% were running at a loss. Between 2014 and 2023, nearly a third of primary grape producers left the industry, with smaller producers hit hardest. Large estates are investing in solar PV and battery storage, while many smaller producers lack the capital and the in-house expertise to do the same.

In this setting, the order of spending matters more than the size of it. Measures that cost little and pay back within a season should come before any project that needs finance.

6. Gaps in energy management practice

The 2016 Stellenbosch University guideline identified four common pitfalls in South African cellars: Not enough time or finance is allocated to energy; improvements focus only on technical measures, so staff don't see their own influence; all the energy knowledge sits with one person; and savings are not measured, so projects appear to have changed nothing. Tariff complexity, which the 2025 restructuring increased, makes all four harder.

None of these needs equipment to fix. A named energy owner, a monthly record of consumption and cost, and a simple measure of kWh per ton of grapes crushed give a cellar the baseline every later decision depends on.

7. Barriers to self-generation

Solar PV is the obvious response to rising tariffs, but it doesn't remove the bill. A grid-tied system still pays the fixed and capacity charges, so its savings come only from the energy charge it displaces. Every embedded generator connected to the grid must also be registered and commissioned. Our SSEG registration guide explains the City of Cape Town process.

Larger projects face a capacity limit. CBN reports that Eskom's 2025 Generation Connection Capacity Assessment shows effectively zero available firm capacity for new large-scale connections in the Western Cape, although about 1,267 MW of privately developed renewable projects in the province, almost all enabled by wheeling, are already operating or under construction. Rural supply also suffers from ageing networks, cable theft and slow repairs, which is why many estates want storage as well as generation.

What South African cellars have already saved

Cellars in the Western Cape have already cut their energy costs, mostly with measures that cost little. The results below come from the case studies in Brent, Silinga and Sanetra (2016). The rand values reflect the tariffs of the time, and some were calculated at R1 per kWh, so at today's tariffs most of these savings would be larger.

Measured and calculated savings in South African cellars, Journal of Energy in Southern Africa, 2016
MeasureResult
Raise a 70,000 litre chilled water tank from 4 °C to the 6 °C the process neededAbout R140,000 a year
Halve the chilled water storage volume in the off-seasonAbout R130,950 a year
Insulate chilled water pipeworkPayback generally under one year
Lower the compressed air set point from 8.5 bar to 7.5 barAbout 7% of compressed air energy
Repair at least 70% of compressed air leaksAbout 10,000 kWh over a harvest season
Replace an electric element water heater with a heat pumpThe heat pump uses about 30% of the element's electricity
Fit a variable speed drive to a 5.5 kW chilled water circulation pumpAbout 25% of the pump's energy
Install a 132 kWp grid-tied solar PV systemPayback in six to seven years

The list is a good example of the right order. The first two measures changed a set point and a tank selection. Only the last one needed real capital, and it came after the cheaper work had reduced the load it had to serve. Across the sector, GreenCape reports that savings of up to two-thirds of energy costs have been achieved in wine.

Operational changes help as well. Durbanville Hills cooled its chilled water reservoir during off-peak hours, and sequenced red wine pump-overs instead of running them together, to avoid creating a demand peak. At the other end of the scale, Vergelegen took its cellar off the grid in 2023 with a ground-mounted solar plant and a 1 MWh battery, a project BusinessTech reported at R13.7 million. The rest of the estate stays connected to the grid.

Where to start: A plan for the next harvest

  1. Review the tariff on every supply point. List each account, its tariff, its NMD and twelve months of charges, split into energy, demand, capacity and fixed charges. Check whether each supply is on the cheapest tariff for the way it is used now.
  2. Profile the harvest. Log the main incomer, and the refrigeration plant if it has its own supply, through at least part of the harvest. The harvest peak sets demand charges and equipment size, and an off-season profile misses it.
  3. Work the chilled water system. Confirm the temperature each process really needs, raise set points to match, use smaller tank volumes when demand drops, insulate pipework and shade the plant.
  4. Fix compressed air. Tag and repair leaks, lower the pressure to what the presses need, and switch off compressors and dryers outside harvest.
  5. Control the pumps. Fit variable speed drives where flow varies, match pumps to the duty, and move irrigation pumping into off-peak and standard periods where the tariff rewards it.
  6. Plan the R22 replacement before it fails. Specify the new plant on the measured cooling load, with an efficiency target and a long-life refrigerant, rather than replacing like for like in an emergency.
  7. Size solar and storage last. Size them on the reduced load and the current tariff structure, so the system doesn't pay for waste you have already removed. Our guide to energy audits before solar explains why.
  8. Keep the data. Monthly kWh, kWh per ton crushed and kWh per kilolitre bottled are the energy inputs a carbon footprint and a buyer's questionnaire ask for.

Questions to ask before you buy a chiller, pump or solar plant

  • Was it sized on our measured harvest load, or on the nameplate of the plant it replaces?
  • What will it save on our tariff, split into energy, demand and fixed charges?
  • Which refrigerant does it use, and how is that refrigerant treated under the Kigali phase-down?
  • How much of the saving could we get from set points, scheduling and leak repair alone?
  • How will we measure the saving after installation, and who reports it?
  • Does the supplier also sell the equipment they are recommending?

The limits of the evidence

The CCC benchmarks are the best data available, but they come from a self-selected sample that does not yet represent the whole industry. Most peer-reviewed research on South African winery energy use dates from 2006 to 2016. No industry-wide energy study has been published since the 2025 tariff restructuring, so its effect on an individual cellar needs a site-specific analysis.

Frequently asked questions

How much electricity does a South African winery use per ton of grapes?

The CCC Benchmark Report 2025 gives average cellar cooling of 232 kWh per ton for white wine and 201 kWh per ton for red, plus processing of about 180 and 174 kWh per ton. Bottling adds about 220 to 230 kWh per kilolitre of wine. Your own cellar may differ, so compare your figures before drawing conclusions.

What is the largest electricity load in a wine cellar?

Cooling. Refrigeration accounts for about half of a winery's energy demand, and it peaks during harvest, in the hottest weeks of the year. That peak also drives demand charges and the size of the refrigeration plant.

Does the R22 phase-out affect my cellar?

Yes, if your chillers still run on R22. From 2026, South Africa's R22 import quota falls to 2.5% of previous consumption, and supply may run out within two years. Plan the replacement now, and avoid a like-for-like swap to a high-GWP HFC, because the Kigali Amendment phases those down too.

Do wine farms pay carbon tax?

No. The agriculture, forestry and other land use sector remains excluded from the carbon tax under Phase 2, which started on 1 January 2026. Wine farms still feel it indirectly, through higher fuel and electricity prices.

Will solar PV solve a winery's electricity costs?

Not on its own. A grid-tied system still pays the fixed and capacity charges, which are now a larger share of the bill. Cut waste and review the tariff first, then size solar and storage on the reduced load and the current tariff structure.
